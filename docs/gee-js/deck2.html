1 · Cloud-free composite

The problem, and it is ours specifically

Half the year, Indonesia is under cloud.

One image is a lottery. So we do not use one image — we take every image over a period and keep, per pixel, the ones that were clear.

A composite is not a photograph

No single moment exists in it. Every pixel may come from a different day, and that is the point.

What to watch for

.filterDate('2024-01-01', '2024-12-31')
.filterBounds(aoi)
.map(maskS2clouds)
.median()
  • too short a period → holes where it was never clear
  • too long → you average across real change
  • .median() rather than .mean() — one bright cloud edge does not drag the answer

Why max: 3000, not 255

Sentinel-2 reflectance is stored scaled — values run to several thousand, not 0–255.

Set max: 255 and almost everything saturates white. Set it to 3000 and the land looks right.

This is a display choice, not a correction. The numbers underneath never changed — you are choosing how to stretch them onto a screen.

2 · NDVI

One subtraction, and why it works

NDVI  =  ( NIR − Red )  ÷  ( NIR + Red )

Yesterday: vegetation reflects ~5% of red and ~50% of near infrared. NDVI is just that gap, normalised so brightness cancels.

A shaded hillside and a sunlit one give the same NDVI for the same plant. That is why we divide.

Reading the number

NDVI usually
< 0 water
0 – 0.2 bare soil, built-up
0.2 – 0.5 grass, crops, shrub
> 0.5 dense vegetation

Ranges overlap and shift by season and sensor. Treat them as a starting point to check, never as a classification.

3 · Classification

The step nobody can copy from me

Everything else today you can paste. Training points you must draw yourself, because they describe your district.

  • 20–40 points per class is enough to start
  • spread them out — not all from one corner
  • pick pixels you are certain about; a doubtful point teaches the classifier something false

Then 70 / 30

Train on 70% of your points. Hold back 30% and let the classifier answer those blind.

If you test on the points you trained with, you measure memory, not skill — and it will look excellent.

Read the accuracy before you look at the map

In that order. The map is persuasive whether or not it is right.

Because this always looks convincing

Crisp boundaries. Sensible colours. None of that is evidence.

Where your error will be

Look again at the middle panel: vegetation and water sit far apart, bare soil and settlement overlap.

Both are dry, bright, moderate in the infrared. Their numbers genuinely resemble each other.

When accuracy stalls around 80%

It is almost never the algorithm. Find the two classes sitting on top of each other, then either add a band that separates them — shortwave infrared often does — or merge them and say so in your report.

4 · Change detection

Two dates, one subtraction

Build the same composite for two years. Subtract the NDVIs.

Negative means vegetation lost between the two dates.

−0.15 is a choice, not a truth

Move the threshold and the area of “change” moves with it. Report the number you used, and show what happens at one step either side.

Change that is not change

Before believing any loss patch, rule out:

  • different season — a harvested paddy is not deforestation
  • cloud shadow in one of the two composites
  • one bad year — drought looks like clearing at this resolution

The honest check is a third date. If the loss persists, it is real; if it returns, it was a season.

5 · Export

Export does not export

Running the line creates a task. Nothing leaves Earth Engine until you open the Tasks tab and press Run.

The most common lost half-hour of the day

People run the script, see no error, and wait for a file that was never requested.

And this is where the quota bites

You are asking for real pixels now, not a picture of them.

  • set scale deliberately — 10 m over a whole province is enormous
  • set region to your district, not the map view
  • maxPixels exists to stop an accident, not to be raised reflexively

Exports land in your Google Drive, and can take minutes to hours.
